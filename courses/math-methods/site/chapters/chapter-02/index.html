2.1Introduction to Complex Numbers

Video Lecture 4, Part 1: Complex Numbers (0:00)

Motivation

In algebra, the quadratic formula

z = − b ± b 2 − 4 a c 2 a (2.1)

provides the general solution of a quadratic equation

a z 2 + b z + c = 0. (2.2)

When the discriminant

d = b 2 − 4 a c (2.3)

is negative, the square root involves a negative number. To extend the number system so that such roots exist, we introduce a new quantity:

i = − 1 , i 2 = − 1. (2.4)
DefinitionComplex Numbers

A complex number is any number of the form

z = x + i y , (2.5)

where x , y ∈ ℝ and i is the imaginary unit defined by i 2 = − 1 .

  • x is the real part of z : Re ( z ) = x .

  • y is the imaginary part of z : Im ( z ) = y .

Both x and y may be positive, negative, or zero.

Special Cases

  • If y = 0 , then z is purely real: z = x .

  • If x = 0 and y ≠ 0 , then z is purely imaginary: z = i y .

Example

  1. z = 4 + 3 i : Re ( z ) = 4 , Im ( z ) = 3 .

  2. z = − 5 i : Re ( z ) = 0 , Im ( z ) = − 5 (pure imaginary).

  3. z = 7 : Re ( z ) = 7 , Im ( z ) = 0 (pure real).

  4. z = − 2 + i 5 : Re ( z ) = − 2 , Im ( z ) = 5 .

Example

Express − 50 in the form x + i y .

Solution.

− 50 = − 1 ⋅ 25 ⋅ 2 = − 1 ⋅ 25 ⋅ 2 = i ⋅ 5 2 = 5 2 i . (2.6)

Motivation from Algebra

Consider

z 2 − 2 z + 2 = 0. (2.7)

Applying the quadratic formula:

z = 2 ± 4 − 8 2 = 2 ± − 4 2 = 2 ± 2 i 2 = 1 ± i . (2.8)

The solutions 1 ± i are complex numbers with both real and imaginary parts nonzero.

Example

Solve z 2 + z + 1 = 0 .

Solution.

z = − 1 ± 1 − 4 2 = − 1 ± − 3 2 = − 1 ± i 3 2 . (2.9)

Thus, the solutions are

z = − 1 2 + i 3 2 and z = − 1 2 − i 3 2 . (2.10)

2.1.4The Complex Plane

Video Lecture 4, Part 2: The Complex Plane and Polar Form (2:48)

DefinitionGeometric Interpretation of Complex Numbers

A complex number z = x + i y can be represented as a point ( x , y ) in a two-dimensional coordinate plane, called the complex plane or Argand diagram.

  • The horizontal axis represents the real part x = Re ( z ) .

  • The vertical axis represents the imaginary part y = Im ( z ) .

RemarkRectangular Form

When we write z = x + i y , we say z is in rectangular form, because ( x , y ) are the rectangular coordinates of the point representing z .

RemarkPolar Form

We can also locate a point using polar coordinates ( r , θ ) :

x = r cos ⁡ ( θ ) , y = r sin ⁡ ( θ ) . (2.11)

Then

z = x + i y = r ( cos ⁡ ( θ ) + i sin ⁡ ( θ ) ) . (2.12)

Later we will see that cos ⁡ ( θ ) + i sin ⁡ ( θ ) = e i θ , giving the compact form

z = r e i θ . (2.13)

Diagram of the Complex Plane

Radians vs Degrees

In calculus and advanced mathematics, radians are the standard measure for angles.

  • Many formulas, such as d d x sin ⁡ ( x ) = cos ⁡ ( x ) , are valid only if x is in radians.

  • For computation, degrees can be used if the context allows, but be careful when substituting into formulas derived with radians.

Worked Examples

Example

Represent 1 + i 3 in polar form.

Solution.

r = 1 2 + ( 3 ) 2 = 1 + 3 = 2 , (2.14)
θ = arctan ⁡ ( 3 1 ) = π 3 ( first quadrant, so the calculator’s angle is right ) . (2.15)

Thus:

1 + i 3 = 2 ( cos ⁡ ( π 3 ) + i sin ⁡ ( π 3 ) ) = 2 e i π / 3 . (2.16)
Example

Convert 3 e − i π / 2 to rectangular form.

Solution.

cos ⁡ ( − π 2 ) = 0 , sin ⁡ ( − π 2 ) = − 1. (2.17)

So:

3 e − i π / 2 = 3 ( 0 − i ) = − 3 i . (2.18)

Terminology and Notation

DefinitionStandard Notation for Complex Numbers

For a complex number

z = x + i y = r ( cos ⁡ ( θ ) + i sin ⁡ ( θ ) ) = r e i θ , (2.19)

we write:

Re ( z ) = x , Im ( z ) = y , | z | = r = x 2 + y 2 , arg ⁡ ( z ) = θ . (2.20)

The angle is only fixed up to whole turns, since θ and θ + 2 π name the same point. So arg ⁡ ( z ) is multivalued: it stands for the whole family θ + 2 n π , n ∈ ℤ . The principal argument, written with a capital A , is the one member of that family lying in a chosen half-open interval of length 2 π :

Arg ( z ) = Θ ∈ ( − π , π ] , so that z = | z | e i Θ . (2.21)
RemarkA notation worth keeping straight

Throughout this chapter, lower case means all values and upper case means the principal one:

arg ⁡ ( z ) = Arg ( z ) + 2 n π , ln ⁡ ( z ) = Ln ( z ) + 2 n π i , n ∈ ℤ . (2.22)

The distinction looks pedantic now, but it is exactly what makes ln ⁡ ( − 1 ) and i i have infinitely many values later in the chapter.

RemarkGetting the quadrant right

A calculator's arctan ⁡ ( y / x ) always returns an angle in ( − π / 2 , π / 2 ) , so it cannot tell z from − z : the points 1 + i and − 1 − i give the same ratio y / x = 1 . Always sketch z first. For x > 0 the calculator's angle is right; for x < 0 turn it by π , adding π when y ≥ 0 and subtracting π when y < 0 , which keeps the answer in ( − π , π ] :

Arg ( − 1 − i ) = arctan ⁡ ( 1 ) − π = π 4 − π = − 3 π 4 . (2.23)

For x = 0 the ratio is undefined; then θ = π / 2 if y > 0 and θ = − π / 2 if y < 0 .

2.1.9Complex Conjugate

DefinitionComplex Conjugate

The complex conjugate of a complex number z = x + i y is defined by

z ― = x − i y . (2.24)

Geometrically, z ― is the reflection of the point z across the real axis in the complex plane.

Basic Identities

For all z , w ∈ ℂ :

z z ― = | z | 2 , z + w ― = z ― + w ― , z w ― = z ― w ― , ( z w ) ― = z ― w ―   ( w ≠ 0 ) . (2.25)

Useful reconstructions:

Re ( z ) = z + z ― 2 , Im ( z ) = z − z ― 2 i . (2.26)

Quick Facts

  • Multiplication/division in polar form:

    ( r 1 e i θ 1 ) ( r 2 e i θ 2 ) = ( r 1 r 2 ) e i ( θ 1 + θ 2 ) , r 1 e i θ 1 r 2 e i θ 2 = ( r 1 r 2 ) e i ( θ 1 − θ 2 ) . (2.31)

    Geometrically, multiplying by r 2 e i θ 2 stretches by r 2 and rotates by θ 2 . Multiplying by i = e i π / 2 is a quarter turn, and ( 1 + i ) 2 = ( 2 e i π / 4 ) 2 = 2 e i π / 2 = 2 i : the length is squared and the angle doubled.

  • Conjugation in polar form: r e i θ ― = r e − i θ .

  • Equality: z 1 = z 2 ⟺ Re ( z 1 ) = Re ( z 2 )   and   Im ( z 1 ) = Im ( z 2 ) .

2.2Complex Algebra

Video Lecture 4, Part 3: Conjugates and Complex Algebra (7:14)

In this section, we develop the algebraic techniques needed to manipulate complex numbers in both rectangular form x + i y and polar form r e i θ . The key operations include:

  • addition and subtraction,

  • multiplication and division,

  • complex conjugation and absolute value,

  • converting between x + i y and r e i θ ,

  • solving equations involving complex numbers,

  • interpreting algebraic relationships geometrically in the complex plane.

These tools allow us to treat complex numbers as a natural extension of real numbers while benefiting from their geometric interpretation.

2.2.4Complex Equations

Video Lecture 4, Part 4: Complex Equations (11:25)

Two complex numbers are equal iff their real parts are equal and their imaginary parts are equal. Often, write z = x + i y and split into real/imaginary equations. In general, if f ( x , y ) and g ( x , y ) are real functions of the real unknowns x and y , then

f ( x , y ) + i g ( x , y ) = 0 ⟺ f ( x , y ) = 0    and    g ( x , y ) = 0 , (2.37)

because a complex number is zero only when both its real and imaginary parts are zero: one complex equation is two real equations.

Example

Solve ( x + i y ) 2 = 2 i for real x , y .

Solution.

( x + i y ) 2 = ( x 2 − y 2 ) + i ( 2 x y ) = 0 + 2 i (2.38)

Equate real and imaginary parts:

{ x 2 − y 2 = 0 , 2 x y = 2. (2.39)

From x 2 − y 2 = ( x − y ) ( x + y ) = 0 we have y = x or y = − x .

Case 1: y = x . Then 2 x y = 2 ⇒ 2 x 2 = 2 ⇒ x 2 = 1 . Hence x = ± 1 and y = x . This gives 1 + i and − 1 − i .

Case 2: y = − x . Then 2 x y = 2 ⇒ 2 x ( − x ) = 2 ⇒ − 2 x 2 = 2 ⇒ x 2 = − 1 , which is impossible for real x . So no solutions from this branch.

Therefore the solutions are

( x , y ) = ( 1 , 1 ) and ( x , y ) = ( − 1 , − 1 ) , (2.40)

i.e. z = 1 + i and z = − 1 − i .

Check. ( 1 + i ) 2 = 1 + 2 i − 1 = 2 i and ( − 1 − i ) 2 = 1 + 2 i − 1 = 2 i .

(Alternative via polar form.) 2 i = 2 e i π / 2 . Square roots have modulus 2 and arguments π 4 and π 4 + π , giving 2 e i π / 4 = 1 + i and 2 e i 5 π / 4 = − 1 − i .

(Picture.) In the x y -plane, which is the complex plane, the real equation x 2 = y 2 is the pair of lines y = ± x , and the imaginary equation x y = 1 is a hyperbola in the first and third quadrants. The solutions are where the curves meet: exactly the two points ( 1 , 1 ) and ( − 1 , − 1 ) . The line y = − x never meets the hyperbola, which is why Case 2 gave nothing. The two square roots are opposite points, ± ( 1 + i ) , as square roots always are.

Identify loci via z = x + i y , | z | = x 2 + y 2 , and distances:

Re z = c   is the vertical line  x = c ; Im z = c   is the horizontal line  y = c . (2.42)

2.3Complex Infinite Series

Video Lecture 4, Part 5: Complex Infinite Series (14:43)

DefinitionComplex Series

A complex series is an expression of the form

∑ n = 0 ∞ z n , z n ∈ ℂ . (2.43)

Let

S N = ∑ n = 0 N z n (2.44)

be the sequence of partial sums. If the limit

lim N → ∞ S N = S (2.45)

exists and is a finite complex number S , then the series converges, and we write

∑ n = 0 ∞ z n = S . (2.46)

If the limit does not exist or is not finite, the series diverges.

TheoremConvergence of Complex Series

Let z n = a n + i b n with a n , b n ∈ ℝ . Then

∑ n = 0 ∞ z n   converges ⟺ ∑ n = 0 ∞ a n   and   ∑ n = 0 ∞ b n   both converge . (2.47)

In that case, the sum satisfies

∑ n = 0 ∞ z n = ∑ n = 0 ∞ a n + i ∑ n = 0 ∞ b n . (2.48)
Remark

In practice, check convergence of the real and imaginary parts separately, or use absolute convergence to simplify the test.

TheoremAbsolute Convergence in ℂ

A complex series ∑ z n is said to be absolutely convergent if the series

∑ n = 0 ∞ | z n | (2.49)

converges, where

| z n | = ( Re z n ) 2 + ( Im z n ) 2 . (2.50)

If ∑ | z n | converges, then the complex series ∑ z n also converges. In other words,

absolute convergence   ⟹   convergence . (2.51)

Moreover, any standard convergence test for real, non-negative series may be applied to the series ∑ | z n | .

Worked Examples

ExampleRatio Test

Test

∑ n = 0 ∞ ( 1 + i ) n 2 n . (2.52)

Here

ρ = lim n → ∞ | ( 1 + i ) n + 1 / 2 n + 1 ( 1 + i ) n / 2 n | = | 1 + i 2 | = 2 2 < 1. (2.53)

Thus the series converges absolutely (and hence converges). In fact it is a geometric series with ratio q = 1 + i 2 , so its sum is

∑ n = 0 ∞ ( 1 + i 2 ) n = 1 1 − q = 2 1 − i = 1 + i . (2.54)

In the plane the partial sums spiral into the point 1 + i : each new term is the previous one turned by 45 ∘ and shrunk by the factor 1 / 2 (Figure 2.3, left).

ExampleAlternating parts

Test

∑ n = 1 ∞ i n n . (2.55)

The terms cycle through i , − 1 , − i , 1 , … . Real part: ∑ ± 1 n alternating — converges by the alternating series test. Imaginary part: ∑ ± 1 n alternating — also converges. Hence the series converges (conditionally, not absolutely).

Written out exactly, with i 2 = − 1 , i 3 = − i , i 4 = 1 , the even terms give the real part and the odd terms the imaginary part:

∑ n = 1 ∞ i n n = ∑ n = 1 ∞ ( − 1 ) n 2 n + i ∑ n = 1 ∞ ( − 1 ) n − 1 2 n − 1 . (2.56)

Both are alternating series whose terms decrease to zero, while ∑ | z n | = ∑ 1 / n is a divergent p -series ( p = 1 2 ). The ratio test cannot decide here: ρ = | i | = i i ¯ = 1 . (Note that | i | = 1 , not i : a modulus is never imaginary.) In the plane the partial sums turn by a quarter at every step and close in only slowly, which is what conditional convergence looks like (Figure 2.3, right).

ExampleGeometric Series
∑ n = 0 ∞ ( r e i θ ) n (2.57)

converges iff | r | < 1 , in which case

∑ n = 0 ∞ ( r e i θ ) n = 1 1 − r e i θ . (2.58)

Quick Facts

For the ratio test applied to ∑ z n :

  • If ρ < 1 , the series converges absolutely.

  • If ρ > 1 , the series diverges.

  • If ρ = 1 , the test is inconclusive — real and imaginary parts must be checked separately.

2.4Complex Power Series: Disk of Convergence

Video Lecture 4, Part 6: The Disk of Convergence (20:39)

DefinitionComplex Power Series

A complex power series centered at z 0 has the form

∑ n = 0 ∞ c n ( z − z 0 ) n , c n ∈ ℂ . (2.59)

It converges for all z within some disk centered at z 0 , and diverges for all z outside that disk.

2.4.1Radius and Disk of Convergence

TheoremRadius of Convergence (Ratio Test)

If

ρ = lim n → ∞ | c n + 1 c n | exists, then R = 1 ρ , (2.60)

with conventions:

ρ = 0 ⇒ R = ∞ , ρ = ∞ ⇒ R = 0. (2.61)

A complex power series converges absolutely when

| z − z 0 | < R , (2.62)

and diverges when

| z − z 0 | > R . (2.63)

The set

{ z ∈ ℂ : | z − z 0 | < R } (2.64)

is called the disk of convergence. On the boundary | z − z 0 | = R , convergence must be tested separately. With z = x + i y and z 0 = x 0 + i y 0 , the condition | z − z 0 | < R reads

( x − x 0 ) 2 + ( y − y 0 ) 2 < R 2 , (2.65)

the inside of the circle of radius R about z 0 . (The right side is R 2 , not R : the left side is a squared distance.) The interval a − R < x < a + R of a real power series is simply the part of this disk that lies on the real axis.

ExampleMaclaurin Series for ln ⁡ ( 1 + z )
ln ⁡ ( 1 + z ) = ∑ n = 1 ∞ ( − 1 ) n + 1 n z n . (2.66)

Ratio test:

ρ = lim n → ∞ | ( − 1 ) n + 2 z n + 1 / ( n + 1 ) ( − 1 ) n + 1 z n / n | = lim n → ∞ | z | n n + 1 = | z | . (2.67)

Thus R = 1 , so the disk of convergence is | z | < 1 .

The boundary circle. On | z | = 1 write z = e i θ ; the terms ( − 1 ) n + 1 e i n θ / n have modulus 1 / n → 0 . For a complex series that is not enough, and the alternating series test (a test for real series) does not apply to complex terms. Test points instead:

  • z = 1 : 1 − 1 2 + 1 3 − ⋯ is alternating and converges, to ln ⁡ ( 2 ) .

  • z = − 1 : every term is ( − 1 ) n + 1 ( − 1 ) n n = − 1 n , so the series is − ( 1 + 1 2 + 1 3 + ⋯ ) and diverges, as it must, since ln ⁡ ( 1 + z ) is ln ⁡ ( 0 ) there.

  • z = i : the real part is ∑ n = 1 ∞ ( − 1 ) n + 1 2 n = 1 2 ln ⁡ ( 2 ) and the imaginary part is ∑ n = 1 ∞ ( − 1 ) n − 1 2 n − 1 = π 4 , both alternating, so the series converges, to

    ln ⁡ ( 1 + i ) = 1 2 ln ⁡ ( 2 ) + i π 4 = ln ( 2 e i π / 4 ) , (2.68)

    which is exactly the logarithm of 1 + i = 2 e i π / 4 .

In fact the series converges at every point of the circle except z = − 1 (a finer test, Dirichlet's, shows it). One bad point on the circle is what limits the radius to 1 (Figure 2.4).

ExampleShifted Center
∑ n = 0 ∞ ( z + 1 − i ) n 3 n . (2.69)

Ratio test:

ρ = lim n → ∞ | ( z + 1 − i ) n + 1 / 3 n + 1 ( z + 1 − i ) n / 3 n | = | z + 1 − i | 3 . (2.70)

Thus convergence requires | z + 1 − i | < 3 . The disk is centered at z 0 = − 1 + i with radius R = 3 .

RemarkProperties of the Disk of Convergence

  • If R = ∞ , the power series converges for all z ∈ ℂ . In this case, the function defined by the series is called entire.

  • If R = 0 , the series converges only at the center z = z 0 .

  • For all z with | z − z 0 | < R , convergence is uniform on compact subsets of the disk. Term-by-term differentiation and integration are therefore valid within this region (see Theorem in Section 2.4).

2.5Euler's Formula

Video Lecture 4, Part 7: Euler's Formula (25:15)

TheoremEuler's Formula

For any real angle θ ,

e i θ = cos ⁡ ( θ ) + i sin ⁡ ( θ ) . (2.71)

This identity connects the complex exponential with the trigonometric functions and plays a central role in oscillations, waves, quantum mechanics, and electrical engineering.

Proof Using Power Series

RemarkStrategy of the Proof

We will:

  1. Start from the power series definition of e x and substitute x = i θ .

  2. Expand ( i θ ) n and separate the terms with even and odd n .

  3. Simplify powers of i and identify the real and imaginary parts with the known series for cos ⁡ ( θ ) and sin ⁡ ( θ ) .

Step 1. Start from the series for e x .

For any real (or complex) number x ,

e x = ∑ n = 0 ∞ x n n ! . (2.72)

Substitute x = i θ (with θ ∈ ℝ ):

e i θ = ∑ n = 0 ∞ ( i θ ) n n ! = ( i θ ) 0 0 ! + ( i θ ) 1 1 ! + ( i θ ) 2 2 ! + ( i θ ) 3 3 ! + ( i θ ) 4 4 ! + ⋯ . (2.73)

Step 2. Write out the first few terms explicitly.

e i θ = 1 + i θ + ( i θ ) 2 2 ! + ( i θ ) 3 3 ! + ( i θ ) 4 4 ! + ( i θ ) 5 5 ! + ⋯ . (2.74)

Now expand the powers of i :

i 0 = 1 , i 1 = i , i 2 = − 1 , i 3 = − i , i 4 = 1 , i 5 = i ,   … (2.75)

So we obtain:

e i θ = 1 + i θ + i 2 θ 2 2 ! + i 3 θ 3 3 ! + i 4 θ 4 4 ! + i 5 θ 5 5 ! + ⋯ = 1 + i θ + ( − 1 ) θ 2 2 ! + ( − i ) θ 3 3 ! + ( 1 ) θ 4 4 ! + ( i ) θ 5 5 ! + ⋯ . (2.76)

Step 3. Group real and imaginary terms.

Group all the terms without i (real part) and all the terms with a factor i (imaginary part):

e i θ = ( 1 − θ 2 2 ! + θ 4 4 ! − θ 6 6 ! + ⋯ ) ⏟ real part + i ( θ − θ 3 3 ! + θ 5 5 ! − θ 7 7 ! + ⋯ ) ⏟ imaginary part . (2.77)

Thus we can write

e i θ = A ( θ ) + i B ( θ ) , (2.78)

where

A ( θ ) = 1 − θ 2 2 ! + θ 4 4 ! − θ 6 6 ! + ⋯ , (2.79)
B ( θ ) = θ − θ 3 3 ! + θ 5 5 ! − θ 7 7 ! + ⋯ . (2.80)

Step 4. Recall the power series for cos ⁡ ( θ ) and sin ⁡ ( θ ) .

From earlier results on Maclaurin series:

cos ⁡ ( θ ) = ∑ n = 0 ∞ ( − 1 ) n θ 2 n ( 2 n ) ! = 1 − θ 2 2 ! + θ 4 4 ! − θ 6 6 ! + ⋯ , (2.81)
sin ⁡ ( θ ) = ∑ n = 0 ∞ ( − 1 ) n θ 2 n + 1 ( 2 n + 1 ) ! = θ − θ 3 3 ! + θ 5 5 ! − θ 7 7 ! + ⋯ . (2.82)

Comparing term by term, we see

A ( θ ) = cos ⁡ ( θ ) , B ( θ ) = sin ⁡ ( θ ) . (2.83)

Therefore,

e i θ = A ( θ ) + i B ( θ ) = cos ⁡ ( θ ) + i sin ⁡ ( θ ) , (2.84)

which completes the proof.

Geometric Meaning

Euler’s formula shows that e i θ lies on the unit circle in the complex plane:

| e i θ | = 1 , arg ⁡ ( e i θ ) = θ . (2.85)

Thus multiplying by e i θ corresponds to a rotation by angle θ about the origin.

The series itself can be watched as it adds up. Each term of ∑ ( i θ ) n / n ! is the previous one multiplied by i θ / n : turned by a quarter turn (the factor i ) and rescaled. For θ = π the partial sums walk 1 , then up by π , then left by π 2 / 2 , then down, then right, in steps that eventually shrink because n ! wins, and they close in on the point − 1 of the unit circle: e i π = cos ⁡ ( π ) + i sin ⁡ ( π ) = − 1 .

Applications of Euler's Formula

RemarkPolar Form

Any nonzero complex number z can be written as

z = r e i θ , r = | z | ,   θ = arg ⁡ ( z ) . (2.86)

This is the polar form of z .

RemarkMultiplication and Division

If z 1 = r 1 e i θ 1 and z 2 = r 2 e i θ 2 , then

z 1 z 2 = r 1 r 2 e i ( θ 1 + θ 2 ) , z 1 z 2 = r 1 r 2 e i ( θ 1 − θ 2 ) . (2.87)

Thus multiplication adds angles, and division subtracts angles.

RemarkRoots of Unity

2.14Applications of Complex Numbers

Complex numbers were introduced in this chapter to make − 1 meaningful, which sounds like a formal convenience. Their real value in physics is different and larger: oscillation and rotation are the same thing, and e i θ is the object that says so. Every application below is a consequence of that single fact.

  • Oscillations and waves. Any quantity varying as A cos ⁡ ( ω t + ϕ ) is the real part of A e i ϕ e i ω t . Adding oscillations becomes adding complex numbers (Section 2.14.2).

  • AC circuits. Differentiation becomes multiplication by i ω , so differential equations become algebra and impedance replaces resistance.

  • Quantum mechanics. The wavefunction is complex by nature, not by convenience. A free particle is ψ ∼ e i ( k x − ω t ) , and the phase carries the interference that produces all quantum behaviour (Chapters 12–13).

  • Fourier analysis. Chapter 7 writes periodic functions as sums of e i n ω t , which is far cleaner than working with sin and cos separately.

  • Stability and normal modes. Solutions behaving as e λ t with complex λ oscillate at rate Im λ and grow or decay at rate Re λ , so a single complex number encodes both (Chapters 3 and 8).

2.14.1Oscillation Is Rotation: Phasors

Video Lecture 6, Part 1: Oscillation Is Rotation (0:00)

Euler's formula, e i ω t = cos ⁡ ( ω t ) + i sin ⁡ ( ω t ) , has a simple geometric meaning. As the time t increases, the complex number z ( t ) = e i ω t keeps the length | z | = 1 while its angle ω t grows steadily, so z moves around the unit circle at the constant angular speed ω . Its real part, the shadow of the moving point on the real axis, is cos ⁡ ( ω t ) . In other words, an oscillation is the shadow of a rotation (Figure 2.14).

This observation turns every oscillation into a complex number. An oscillation of amplitude A and phase ϕ can be written as

A cos ⁡ ( ω t + ϕ ) = Re [ A e i ( ω t + ϕ ) ] = Re [ ( A e i ϕ ) e i ω t ] . (2.176)

The factor e i ω t is the same for every oscillation of frequency ω ; everything that distinguishes one oscillation from another is contained in the complex number A e i ϕ , called the phasor of the oscillation. Its modulus is the amplitude and its argument is the phase. For example, the oscillation 2 cos ⁡ ( ω t + π 3 ) has the phasor 2 e i π / 3 = 1 + i 3 .

Phasors also simplify calculus. Differentiating the rotating arrow gives

d d t e i ω t = i ω e i ω t , (2.177)

so the derivative is obtained by multiplying by i ω . Since i = e i π / 2 , multiplying by i turns an arrow by 90 ∘ without changing its length. This is why the derivative of cos ⁡ ( ω t ) , namely − ω sin ⁡ ( ω t ) = ω cos ⁡ ( ω t + π 2 ) , is the same oscillation shifted forward by a quarter period. Differentiation becomes multiplication, and differential equations for oscillations become algebraic equations for phasors. The rest of this section uses this one idea in different settings.

2.14.2Adding Waves

Video Lecture 6, Part 2: Adding Waves (3:08)

Two oscillations of the same frequency add in the simplest possible way: their phasors add. The first example below shows this in general, and the second works it out for a concrete pair of waves.

ExamplePhysics: adding two waves of the same frequency

Two sources produce oscillations of the same frequency ω but different amplitudes and phases,

y 1 ( t ) = A 1 cos ⁡ ( ω t + ϕ 1 ) , y 2 ( t ) = A 2 cos ⁡ ( ω t + ϕ 2 ) . (2.178)

Find their sum. This is the central calculation in interference and in AC circuit analysis.

Solution.

Done with trigonometric identities this is tedious. The complex method is to notice that, by Euler's formula, A cos ⁡ ( ω t + ϕ ) is the real part of A e i ( ω t + ϕ ) . So write each wave as the real part of a complex exponential and add:

y 1 + y 2 = Re [ A 1 e i ( ω t + ϕ 1 ) + A 2 e i ( ω t + ϕ 2 ) ] = Re [ ( A 1 e i ϕ 1 + A 2 e i ϕ 2 ) e i ω t ] . (2.179)

The time dependence e i ω t factors out completely, and everything specific to the two waves is collected in the bracket

A 1 e i ϕ 1 + A 2 e i ϕ 2 ⏟ a single complex number = A e i ϕ , (2.180)

called the phasor of the combined wave. Adding two oscillations has been reduced to adding two complex numbers — that is, to placing two arrows tip to tail in the complex plane. Reading off the modulus and argument of the sum,

A = | A 1 e i ϕ 1 + A 2 e i ϕ 2 | , ϕ = arg ( A 1 e i ϕ 1 + A 2 e i ϕ 2 ) , (2.181)

and the answer is y 1 + y 2 = A cos ⁡ ( ω t + ϕ ) : the sum is another oscillation at the same frequency, with a new amplitude and phase.

Two special cases are worth noting. If ϕ 1 = ϕ 2 the phasors are parallel and A = A 1 + A 2 — constructive interference. If ϕ 2 = ϕ 1 + π they are antiparallel and A = | A 1 − A 2 | — destructive interference, which vanishes entirely when the amplitudes are equal.

ExampleAdding 3 cos ⁡ ( ω t ) and 4 sin ⁡ ( ω t )

Write 3 cos ⁡ ( ω t ) + 4 sin ⁡ ( ω t ) as a single wave A cos ⁡ ( ω t + ϕ ) .

Solution.

To use phasors, both terms must first be written as cosines. A sine is a cosine delayed by a quarter of a period, sin ⁡ ( ω t ) = cos ⁡ ( ω t − π 2 ) , so the phasor of the first term is 3 and the phasor of the second term is 4 e − i π / 2 = − 4 i . The phasor of the sum is the sum of the phasors,

3 − 4 i . (2.182)

Its modulus is the amplitude of the combined wave, and its argument is the phase:

A = 3 2 + 4 2 = 5 , ϕ = − tan − 1 ⁡ ( 4 3 ) = − 53.13 ∘ . (2.183)

Therefore

3 cos ⁡ ( ω t ) + 4 sin ⁡ ( ω t ) = 5 cos ⁡ ( ω t − 53.13 ∘ ) . (2.184)

The amplitude of the sum is 5 , not 3 + 4 = 7 , because the two waves are a quarter of a period out of step and never reach their maxima at the same time. In general, the amplitude of the sum of two waves follows from the law of cosines applied to the two phasors,

A 2 = A 1 2 + A 2 2 + 2 A 1 A 2 cos ⁡ ( ϕ 2 − ϕ 1 ) , (2.185)

which gives A = A 1 + A 2 for waves in phase and A = | A 1 − A 2 | for waves half a period apart, the two special cases of the previous example; every other phase difference gives an amplitude between these two extremes. One can check the result by expanding the right-hand side: 5 cos ⁡ ( ω t − 53.13 ∘ ) = 5 cos ⁡ ( 53.13 ∘ ) cos ⁡ ( ω t ) + 5 sin ⁡ ( 53.13 ∘ ) sin ⁡ ( ω t ) , and 5 cos ⁡ ( 53.13 ∘ ) = 3 , 5 sin ⁡ ( 53.13 ∘ ) = 4 . Figure 2.15 shows the calculation.

2.14.3AC Circuits: Impedance and Resonance

Video Lecture 6, Part 3: AC Circuits (6:21)

In an alternating-current circuit, the source voltage oscillates as V ( t ) = V 0 cos ⁡ ( ω t ) = Re [ V 0 e i ω t ] , and after a short time every current in the circuit oscillates at the same frequency, I ( t ) = Re [ I 0 e i ω t ] , where the complex amplitude I 0 records both the size of the current and its phase. Each circuit element relates its voltage to its current, and with the factor e i ω t each relation becomes a multiplication:

resistor: V = R I , Z R = R , inductor: V = L d I d t = i ω L I , Z L = i ω L , capacitor: I = C d V d t = i ω C V , Z C = 1 i ω C = − i ω C . (2.186)

The complex number Z , the impedance, plays the role of resistance in Ohm's law V = Z I . The factor i has a physical meaning: multiplying by i = e i π / 2 turns a phasor by a quarter turn. So the voltage across a resistor is in phase with the current, the voltage across an inductor leads the current by a quarter period, and the voltage across a capacitor lags behind it by a quarter period.

For a resistor, an inductor and a capacitor in series, the impedances add:

Z = R + i ( ω L − 1 ω C ) , | I 0 | = V 0 | Z | = V 0 R 2 + ( ω L − 1 ω C ) 2 . (2.189)

Since I 0 = V 0 / Z , the current lags behind the source voltage by the phase angle

arg ⁡ Z = arctan ⁡ ( ω L − 1 / ( ω C ) R ) , (2.190)

which is negative (the current leads) when the capacitor dominates. A differential equation for the current has become arithmetic with complex numbers.

The current is largest when the imaginary part of Z vanishes, that is, when ω L = 1 / ( ω C ) . This happens at the resonance frequency

ω 0 = 1 L C , (2.191)

where | Z | = R . For example, with R = 20   Ω , L = 0.1 H, C = 10   μ F and V 0 = 10 V, the resonance frequency is ω 0 = 1000   s − 1 and the current amplitude there is | I 0 | = 10 / 20 = 0.5 A. At half that frequency, ω = 500   s − 1 , we have ω L = 50   Ω and 1 / ( ω C ) = 200   Ω , so | Z | = 20 2 + 150 2 ≈ 151.3   Ω and | I 0 | ≈ 0.066 A, less than a seventh of its value at resonance. The current is large only near ω 0 (Figure 2.16). This is how a radio selects one station: turning the tuning knob changes C , which moves ω 0 onto the frequency of the desired station, and only that station drives an appreciable current.

2.14.4The Damped Oscillator

Video Lecture 6, Part 4: The Damped Oscillator (9:57)

When friction is present, the exponent in e λ t is no longer purely imaginary. A complex λ then describes two things at once, oscillation and decay, as the following example shows.

ExamplePhysics: the damped oscillator

A mass m on a spring of stiffness k , slowed by a friction force proportional to its velocity, obeys Newton's second law m x ¨ = − k x − b x ˙ , where b is the friction coefficient. Dividing by m , we write the equation as

x ¨ + 2 γ x ˙ + ω 0 2 x = 0 , γ = b 2 m , ω 0 2 = k m . (2.192)

Here ω 0 is the angular frequency the oscillator would have without friction, and γ measures the strength of the friction. Find the motion.

Solution.

We look for a solution of the form x = e λ t , because differentiating an exponential only multiplies it by a constant: x ˙ = λ e λ t and x ¨ = λ 2 e λ t . Substituting into the equation gives

( λ 2 + 2 γ λ + ω 0 2 ) e λ t = 0 . (2.193)

Since e λ t is never zero, λ must satisfy the quadratic equation λ 2 + 2 γ λ + ω 0 2 = 0 , whose roots are

λ = − γ ± γ 2 − ω 0 2 . (2.194)

When the friction is weak, γ < ω 0 , the number under the square root is negative and the roots are complex:

λ = − γ ± i ω d , ω d = ω 0 2 − γ 2 . (2.195)

The physical motion is the real part of A e λ t . Since e λ t = e − γ t e i ω d t , Euler's formula gives

x ( t ) = A e − γ t cos ⁡ ( ω d t ) . (2.196)

The two parts of the complex number λ play separate roles: the real part − γ makes the amplitude decay, and the imaginary part ω d sets the frequency of the oscillation.

For a numerical example, take m = 1 kg, k = 16 N/m and b = 0.5 kg/s. Then ω 0 = 4   s − 1 , γ = 0.25   s − 1 and ω d = 3.992   s − 1 , so the friction hardly changes the frequency. It does, however, steadily reduce the amplitude: after 9 s the amplitude is e − 0.25 ⋅ 9 ≈ 0.105 , about a tenth of its initial value (Figure 2.17).

The complex solution has a simple picture. In the complex plane, e λ t is an arrow that rotates at the angular frequency ω d while its length shrinks as e − γ t , so its tip moves along a spiral toward the origin. The motion x ( t ) is the projection of this arrow onto the real axis. If the friction is strong, γ ≥ ω 0 , the roots λ are real and negative, and the mass returns to equilibrium without oscillating; this is called critical damping when γ = ω 0 and overdamping when γ > ω 0 . Figure 2.17 shows the spiral and, beside it, the damped oscillation it projects.

2.14.5Roots of Unity at Work: Three-Phase Power

Video Lecture 6, Part 5: Roots of Unity at Work (13:32)

The n th roots of unity, the solutions of z n = 1 , are z k = e 2 π i k / n for k = 0 , 1 , … , n − 1 . They lie at the corners of a regular n -sided polygon inscribed in the unit circle, and they have a remarkable property: their sum is zero. To see why, write them as powers of w = e 2 π i / n and sum the geometric series:

1 + w + w 2 + ⋯ + w n − 1 = w n − 1 w − 1 = 0 , (2.197)

because w n = 1 while w ≠ 1 . Geometrically, the n arrows from the origin to the corners of a regular polygon balance one another. For n = 3 , for instance, 1 + ( − 1 2 + 3 2 i ) + ( − 1 2 − 3 2 i ) = 0 .

This fact is used every day in the electric power grid. A three-phase supply provides three voltages of equal amplitude, each delayed by a third of a period with respect to the previous one:

V k ( t ) = V 0 cos ⁡ ( ω t − 2 π k 3 ) = Re [ V 0 w k e i ω t ] , k = 0 , 1 , 2 , w = e − 2 π i / 3 . (2.198)

Their phasors are V 0 , V 0 w and V 0 w 2 : three arrows 120 ∘ apart. Their sum is therefore

V 0 e i ω t ( 1 + w + w 2 ) = 0 (2.199)

at every instant (Figure 2.18). When the three phases drive equal loads, the three currents are also equal phasors 120 ∘ apart, and they too add to zero. The current that would have to return along a shared wire is then zero, which is why a balanced three-phase system can carry power with very little return current.

2.14.6The Free Particle in Quantum Mechanics

Video Lecture 6, Part 6: The Quantum Corkscrew (16:02)

In quantum mechanics a particle is described by a wavefunction, and for a free particle moving along the x axis the wavefunction is

ψ ( x , t ) = e i ( k x − ω t ) = cos ⁡ ( k x − ω t ) + i sin ⁡ ( k x − ω t ) . (2.200)

Here complex numbers are not a convenience: the wavefunction itself is complex. At each point x , ψ is a complex number, which we may draw as an arrow on a small dial (Figure 2.19).

The meaning of the two parts of the exponent becomes clear if we split it as ψ = e i k x e − i ω t . At a fixed point x , the factor e − i ω t is a phasor turning at the rate ω , so every arrow simply turns as time runs. At a fixed time, the factor e i k x turns once every wavelength λ = 2 π / k along x , so neighbouring dials point in different directions. Together, the pattern slides along x : the crests of the real and imaginary parts move at the speed v = ω / k . The size of ψ , on the other hand, is the same everywhere, | ψ | 2 = 1 , so this particle is equally likely to be found anywhere.

The relation between ω and k follows from the Schrödinger equation for a free particle,

i ℏ ∂ ψ ∂ t = − ℏ 2 2 m ∂ 2 ψ ∂ x 2 . (2.201)

Each derivative of the exponential only multiplies it by a constant: ∂ ψ / ∂ t = − i ω ψ , so the left-hand side is i ℏ ( − i ω ) ψ = ℏ ω ψ , and ∂ 2 ψ / ∂ x 2 = ( i k ) 2 ψ = − k 2 ψ , so the right-hand side is ℏ 2 k 2 2 m ψ . Both sides are a number times ψ , and ψ is a solution exactly when

ℏ ω = ℏ 2 k 2 2 m . (2.202)

With the energy E = ℏ ω and the momentum p = ℏ k , this is E = p 2 / 2 m , the kinetic energy of a free particle.

Remark

Notice what happened to the exponential and trigonometric functions in this chapter. Over ℝ they look unrelated: one grows, the others oscillate. Over ℂ they are the same function seen along different directions, tied together by e i z , and cosh and cos differ only by which axis you walk along. Extending to complex numbers did not add complication; it removed a distinction that was never really there.
